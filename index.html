Read me

This is my personal and professional site, a repository for the disparate work, art, and writing I make, plus a short bio and a few notes on how I think about building things. Part catch-all, part brain dump, part business card that got a little out of hand.

It’s a single self-contained HTML file, put together with no templates or site builder, and a place I use to experiment with web design the way I use a sketchbook. The sky, the hill, the custom typeface, and the notes in the margins were all made from scratch, so it’s as much a craft project as a résumé. It’ll keep changing, so treat anything here as a work in progress.

*I was born in Allentown (PA), but have “lived” in Philadelphia (PA), Cambridge (MA), New York (NY), Rome (Italy), and Birmingham (AL). (but not necessarily in that sequential order)

A surprisingly wonderful place to live, especially for someone from the Northeast who knew nothing about it and moved there on 13 days’ notice as part of my Venture for America Fellowship.....

I currently work across operations, strategy, product, and capital fundraising, helping founders and teams get from a rough idea to a real, shipped thing, with a maybe-unhealthy attention to craft and clarity. These days that mostly means a handful of blockchain startups and organizations, plus a venture capital firm. I’m cross-disciplinary by nature, and I’ve filled a lot of roles across the blockchain, real estate, architecture, and property technology industries.

I spend the rest of my time building and backing things I genuinely care about: lately that’s pulled me toward AI, fintech, marketplaces, online communities, and design tools. I’m drawn to products that take the friction out of work, give people room to make things, and bring them together rather than apart, especially the ambitious, start-from-nothing kind that focus on the details.

I have been active in Bitcoin and blockchain since 2017, and I’m especially interested in what the technology can do for real-world assets. I also write for blockchain-focused publications and online figures in the space. (though a lot of what I do there is anonymous or pseudonymous)

I originally trained as an architect, completing my undergraduate degree at Temple University, the Design Discovery program at Harvard University’s GSD, and my master’s at the University of Pennsylvania. I’m no longer one, though I still occasionally design buildings, and I care as much as ever about the built environment: urbanism, placemaking, and the potential to build something better. My research, in particular, looks at how the environments we build feed the loneliness and isolation epidemic of modern life. These days I consider myself more of a “builder.” As Virgil Abloh once said:

I believe a builder (in the sense Marc Andreessen describes) is someone who makes things.....

“Young architects can change the world by not building buildings.”

Website UX/UI Specifications
  • Aa420 · 97Holben “Helvetica” (read more about the custom typeface I designed.....)

    Helvetica is proprietary and can’t be freely embedded on the web, so the text you’re reading isn’t actually Helvetica. It’s a custom cut made specifically for this site, built on Archivo (an open-source face in the same neo-grotesque lineage). The approach borrows from Virgil Abloh’s “3% rule,” his idea that altering something by roughly three percent is enough to make it your own while keeping it familiar. Here, Archivo was narrowed about 3% on its width axis and given bespoke weights, a small, deliberate change that leaves the face recognizably Helvetica-adjacent but unique to this website. (It’s also why the name keeps “Helvetica” in quotation marks.)

  • Aa500 · 600Caveat (handwritten margin notes..... whose handwriting is it?)

    The handwritten notes in the margins (and the plane’s “Hello, World!” banner) are set in Caveat, a handwriting typeface by Pablo Impallari of Impallari Type in Rosario, Argentina, commissioned by Google and free through Google Fonts. It uses OpenType contextual alternates, so a letter changes shape a little depending on where it falls in a word, the way real handwriting never repeats itself exactly. Here it’s set in International Klein Blue, so the notes read as if they were jotted into the margins by hand.

  • #FAF6EAMuted Off White (Moleskine notebook paper by “day,” the body text by “night”)
  • #999999Gray (dates, years & muted notes but no cool explanation. Just Gray.)
  • #000000Black (body text by “day,” paper by “night”..... how black is the blackest black?)

    On a screen, #000000 is as black as it gets: pure black, the floor of the color space. In the physical world, the “blackest black” has been a genuine arms race. Anish Kapoor secured exclusive artistic rights to Vantablack, the light-trapping nanotube coating, so artist Stuart Semple answered by making his own super-blacks available to every artist except Kapoor. His latest, Black 4.0, swallows about 99.96% of visible light, rivaling Vantablack itself. The text you’re reading just borrows the on-screen version of that idea.

  • #0000FFTrue Blue (hyperlinks & interactive elements..... but why are they blue?)

    The story behind the blue link is surprisingly simple: building the Mosaic browser in 1993, Marc Andreessen made hyperlinks blue largely because he liked it, finding it a “nice, firm color” that read clearly against the gray backgrounds and 256-color palettes of early monitors. Every blue link since traces back to that choice.

Yves Klein mixed this exact blue and registered the formula as his own in 1960. You can’t truly own a color, but he came close.....

M.Arch (Master of Architecture) Thesis Proposal
The University of Pennsylvania, Stuart Weitzman School of Design
Academic Year: 2020–2021

The Architecture of Capital:
Designing for a Bitcoin-Backed Future

Linking innovative finance to design quality and creative control in development.

Introduction

Bitcoin, with its unique properties as a scarce, digital, and globally recognized asset, has already begun reshaping the financial landscape. Yet, its potential to transform real estate development remains largely unexplored. My thesis proposes a framework for integrating Bitcoin into real estate development financing, creating a new capital structure that leverages Bitcoin’s long-term appreciation potential and the stability of traditional property assets.

Research Objective

The goal of this thesis is to design a hybrid collateral model where Bitcoin serves as a percentage of the collateral package for development loans. By combining Bitcoin with traditional real estate assets, developers can unlock new sources of capital, hedge against inflation, and maintain exposure to Bitcoin’s upside while still advancing real-world projects. This approach bridges the two historically separate markets of digital assets and physical real estate into a unified financing tool.

Literature Review

Research on Bitcoin’s financial properties before 2020 provides a foundation for exploring its role in real estate. Several studies have examined Bitcoin’s potential as a hedge or safe-haven asset, with mixed results but clear indications of its low correlation with traditional assets (Baur, Hong, & Lee, 2018)1. This characteristic suggests that Bitcoin could enhance portfolio diversification when added to conventional collateral packages.

Additionally, work on real estate finance highlights the importance of diversified collateral structures in improving credit access and reducing lender risk (Garmaise & Moskowitz, 1999)2. If Bitcoin is viewed as “digital gold,” as many early adopters and researchers have suggested (Yermack, 2015)3, then its inclusion in a capital stack could serve as a novel form of hard-money collateral.

Most existing literature on Bitcoin-backed lending has focused on retail lending platforms and margin trading, where collateral is subject to liquidation if asset prices fall. This presents a challenge for long-term, illiquid projects, such as real estate development. There is little to no research (as of 2020) on non-liquidating, institutional-grade Bitcoin collateral models, which presents a unique opportunity for this thesis to contribute original insight.

Hypothesis

Primary hypothesis

Integrating Bitcoin as 10–30% of the collateral package in real estate development loans will improve capital access and project returns without significantly increasing lender risk, provided that the loan structure is designed to mitigate short-term volatility, such as no mark-to-market liquidation triggers.

Secondary hypotheses

  • Developers using Bitcoin-backed financing will experience reduced reliance on outside equity partners and retain a greater share of future profits.
  • The inclusion of Bitcoin will attract a new class of investors seeking yield with Bitcoin exposure, thereby increasing liquidity for development projects.
  • Bitcoin’s long-term price appreciation will positively impact loan performance and encourage longer borrower-lender alignment.

Background and Significance

While Bitcoin is often treated as a passive store of value, its integration into real estate financing could unlock significant economic activity. Consider a hypothetical mixed-use development project valued at $20 million: the developer could secure a $15 million construction loan where $3 million of the collateral package is in Bitcoin, with the remaining $17 million collateralized by the land and future building improvements. This structure would allow the developer to:

  • Reduce reliance on outside equity partners and preserve a greater share of future profits.
  • Maintain exposure to Bitcoin’s price appreciation throughout the project’s lifespan.
  • Align project timelines with Bitcoin’s four-year halving cycles to encourage long-term planning.
  • Attract Bitcoin-native investors seeking exposure to yield-generating real-world assets.

Implications for Architectural Design

This financing model does more than shift capital flows; it has the potential to reshape architectural decision-making and design outcomes in the following ways:

1. Freeing up capital for design quality

By improving access to capital or providing more favorable loan terms, Bitcoin-backed financing can allow developers to allocate more funds toward high-quality materials, sustainability features, and innovative design solutions that are often cut during value engineering.

2. Encouraging long-term thinking

Bitcoin’s ethos of low time preference naturally aligns with architecture that prioritizes durability and longevity. A financing structure that rewards long-term commitment could incentivize projects that use timeless design principles rather than short-lived, purely cost-driven solutions.

3. Empowering architect-developers

Architects acting as developers often struggle to secure financing and must give up significant equity or creative control. A Bitcoin-based collateral strategy could allow them to retain a larger share of their project, preserving design integrity and authorship.

4. Enabling visionary and community-oriented projects

Bitcoin-backed financing can provide opportunities for smaller, community-driven, or experimental projects such as galleries, maker spaces, and cultural centers that may not attract conventional capital. Architects could spearhead these projects and use this financial model to bring visionary ideas to life.

5. Creating a global patronage model for architecture

Bitcoin’s borderless nature opens the door for projects to attract funding from a global network of Bitcoiners who share values of permanence, resilience, and freedom. This could democratize architectural patronage and fuel a new wave of culturally significant projects worldwide.

Methodology and Expected Outcome

This thesis will explore hypothetical capital stack models like the one above, stress-test scenarios under different Bitcoin price movements, and analyze the legal and regulatory considerations of using Bitcoin as collateral. It will examine custody solutions available as of 2020, collateralization ratios, volatility management strategies, and incentive alignment to produce a practical framework for future implementation.

This thesis aims to deliver a replicable framework for real estate developers and architect-developers to incorporate Bitcoin into their financing strategy responsibly. Beyond financial innovation, it seeks to demonstrate how this model can improve design quality, enable long-term thinking, and create opportunities for more visionary and socially valuable projects.

Conclusion

Integrating Bitcoin into real estate financing represents a forward-looking step for capital markets, where digital and physical assets could coexist in sophisticated, mutually reinforcing structures. For architects, it offers the promise of greater creative control, higher-quality outcomes, and new opportunities for cultural and community impact. This thesis will argue that Bitcoin can become not just a tool for capital formation, but a catalyst for better architecture.
